# Hail of Fire versus O Group: An In-Depth Comparison

A narration-friendly, read-aloud analysis comparing two World War Two miniatures rulesets that share a great deal of design DNA: Hail of Fire, by Brandon Fraley, and O Group, by David Brown, published by Reisswitz Press through Too Fat Lardies. Tables have been avoided, dice values are written out, and game names are spelled in full, so a text-to-speech reader can narrate this smoothly.


## Why these two games belong in the same conversation.

At first glance these are different animals. Hail of Fire is a company-level game; you command something like five to eight platoons. O Group is a battalion-level game; you command two or three infantry companies plus their supporting assets, with each base representing a section and the platoon serving as the basic maneuver unit. So O Group sits one full echelon above Hail of Fire. A single Hail of Fire force is, roughly, one of the companies inside an O Group battalion.

And yet, mechanically and philosophically, these two designs are unusually close cousins. Both are, at heart, games about command friction rather than about dice and damage. Both treat fire as something that suppresses far more often than it kills. Both bury the player in fog of war and refuse to give perfect information. Both make the chain of command degrade as casualties mount, so that a battered force becomes not just weaker but harder to control. And both largely reject the points-shopping model of force construction in favor of historical, scenario-driven forces. They arrive at strikingly similar conclusions about what a World War Two tactical game should feel like, while solving the specifics in different ways and at different scales. That is what makes the comparison worthwhile: it is less a contest of opposites than a study of two designers reaching for the same truth from adjacent altitudes.

Let me go through it subsystem by subsystem.


## Force construction.

Hail of Fire is openly point-free. You field platoons, each a default of three squads, guns, or vehicles, with a platoon leader and a handful of optional support weapons. Balance is handled before the game by the Force Parity Procedure, a comparative ranking of the two forces that converts any imbalance into starting Hero Points or Break Points, with the Break Limit, five plus two per unit, as the force's overall staying power.

O Group is also, in effect, point-free, but it gets there from the opposite direction. Rather than balancing two arbitrary forces, it hands you a historical battalion with its supporting assets and builds the game around a scenario. You think about organization, reserves, and supports the way a real battalion commander would, not about squeezing an optimal list out of a points budget. The design explicitly encourages thinking about the allocation of reserves.

My analysis: this is the first and one of the deepest parallels. Both games reject the tournament-style points list as the basis of balance, and both produce forces that look like real orders of battle rather than optimized rosters. The difference is one of method and emphasis. Hail of Fire makes balance an explicit pre-game negotiation between two players, which is portable to any matchup but a little subjective. O Group makes balance a property of the historical scenario, which is more authentic but less suited to a casual pick-up game between two arbitrary collections. Hail of Fire is the better tool for, here are my models and yours, let us make a fair fight. O Group is the better tool for, let us refight a specific battalion action.


## Command and activation. The heart of both games.

This is where the two designs are most alike in spirit and most different in texture.

In Hail of Fire, each turn you roll one die for every platoon leader and every vehicle unit. Each roll of four or more yields one Order Point, and you always add one more for free. You spend those points to activate units, and a leader can move or fire a whole platoon on a single order, or split two orders within it. Critically, leaders who have been shot up generate no die, so a damaged force produces fewer activations. Hero Points, a secret and variable resource, let you interrupt and act out of sequence.

In O Group, the engine is the orders dice pool. Each turn you roll a pool of roughly eight or nine dice, and the results become company orders that you spend to activate platoons to move, fire, conduct reconnaissance, or close with the enemy. The battalion headquarters is the source of orders but is itself immobile, while company commanders are mobile and can each add a die to the pool to improve the command situation. Some command results represent inertia, or actively reduce the effectiveness of the command chain, modeling friction directly. And here is the crucial echo: as the battalion takes casualties, the headquarters loses orders dice, so command and control becomes harder as the battle goes on. You will almost never have enough orders for every unit, which forces you to prioritize.

My analysis: the two activation engines rhyme almost perfectly. Both are order economies, where the scarce resource is not movement or firepower but the commander's attention. Both make that resource shrink as the force is ground down, so that a losing player suffers a command death-spiral, fewer orders on top of fewer troops. This shared idea, that command degrades under attrition, is the single most important thing these two games have in common, and it is something most of the rest of the hobby does not model at all. The differences are matters of scale and flavor. O Group's pool of eight or nine dice, fed by mobile company commanders, models a battalion staff distributing a limited number of orders across companies. Hail of Fire's smaller, leader-by-leader pool, topped up by secret Hero Points, models a company commander pushing his platoons and occasionally spending a burst of heroic initiative. O Group's command layer is richer and more explicitly hierarchical, befitting the higher echelon. Hail of Fire's is lighter and faster, with the Hero Point interrupt adding a jolt of unpredictability that O Group does not really have.


## Movement.

Hail of Fire uses its distinctive declared-then-rolled movement. You name a destination, then roll: infantry move two six-sided dice in inches, guns one die, vehicles three dice. Out of the enemy's sight you simply move your maximum. Short dashes are reliable; long sprints in the open are gambles. This is the game's substitute for reaction fire.

O Group treats movement as an ordered action like any other. To move a platoon meaningfully, you generally spend an order on it, and movement is distinguished by posture, a deliberate tactical advance versus a faster, riskier bound. Because orders are scarce, the friction in O Group movement is not whether you will physically reach your destination, as it is in Hail of Fire, but whether you can spare the command attention to move that unit at all this turn.

My analysis: both games make movement costly, but they price it differently. Hail of Fire prices movement in luck and exposure, you might not roll far enough, and a long move under observation invites fire. O Group prices movement in command, you might not have an order to spare. The result feels similar at the table, a sense that you cannot simply glide everything forward each turn, but the underlying levers are different. Hail of Fire's randomized distance is the more novel idea and the better fit for a fast game without reaction fire. O Group's order-gated movement is the more naturalistic fit for a command game where the bottleneck is the commander's span of control.


## Spotting, hidden units, and fog of war.

This is the subsystem where both games invest the most, and where they diverge most interestingly.

Hail of Fire builds fog of war from several layers. Forces can deploy hidden, with reveal distances governed by terrain and proximity. Recon teams extend those distances. But the truly original layer is internal: because fire generates Received Fire Points that are not resolved until a unit next activates, you do not actually know your own casualties until you are forced to act on the unit. Hail of Fire hides not only the enemy from you, but the state of your own men.

O Group builds its fog of war primarily around spotting the enemy, and it does so with real teeth. Forces deploy hidden. Spotting a hidden unit is itself an action that requires an order, and, decisively, only reconnaissance units and patrols can spot hidden units. That single rule makes reconnaissance indispensable in a way few games achieve; without recon, you are blind, and you cannot simply will the enemy into view. On top of that, O Group resolves observation at the moment of firing. If a target is in the open it is spotted automatically, but if it is in cover you roll a separate spotting die: on a one through three it is spotted, and on a four through five or six it is obscured. The damage table then has two rows, a spotted row and an obscured row, and you roll on whichever the spotting die dictated. So even when you fire, you may discover that you could not really see your target well enough to hurt it.

My analysis: both games are among the foggiest in the hobby, but they locate the fog in different places. O Group's fog is mostly about the enemy: where are they, can my reconnaissance find them, can I even see the unit I am shooting at. Hail of Fire's fog is split, half about the enemy through hidden deployment, and half, uniquely, about yourself through delayed casualty resolution. If you want the purest model of the reconnaissance battle, the agony of advancing into ground you cannot see, O Group is unmatched, and its rule that only recon can lift hidden status is a masterstroke. If you want the specific, eerie sensation of not knowing whether your own platoon is combat-effective until you order it forward, only Hail of Fire delivers that. The two approaches are complementary rather than competing; one could imagine a game that wanted both.


## Shooting and the gun and armour system.

In Hail of Fire, you roll a number of dice equal to your weapon's Rate of Fire, hitting on four or more, with all modifiers applied to the target number rather than the die. Hits become Received Fire Points rather than immediate casualties. Anti-tank fire uses the elegant roll-the-difference method: compare anti-tank value to armor, and if anti-tank exceeds armor you roll dice equal to the difference, with any six destroying the vehicle; if it does not exceed armor you can only suppress; and if armor is at least double the anti-tank value, the weapon cannot hurt the target at all.

O Group's firing is bound up with its spotting, as just described, and with its shock system, described next. Its gun and armour interaction for vehicles is more historically textured than Hail of Fire's single comparison, taking into account the quality of the shot through that spotted-or-obscured distinction, and resolving penetration against armor in a way that preserves the period feel of which guns could hurt which tanks at which ranges. Infantry and support fire, meanwhile, is overwhelmingly about generating shock rather than immediately removing stands.

My analysis: Hail of Fire's shooting is the more abstract and the more elegant, with its roll-the-difference penetration being, in my view, one of the cleanest anti-tank rules at any scale, and its single unifying mechanic, four or more with modifiers to the target number, running through the entire game. O Group's shooting is more textured and more tightly woven into its spotting, so that the very act of firing tests whether you could see well enough to matter. Neither is trying to be a ballistic simulator on the order of Battlegroup, but O Group leans a little more toward historical texture, while Hail of Fire leans toward speed and unity of mechanic.


## Suppression. Shock versus Received Fire Points.

This is the deepest single parallel between the two games, because both are built on the conviction that suppression, not lethality, is the real product of fire.

In O Group, fire causes a platoon to accumulate shock. As shock builds, the platoon degrades, passing through suppression, and only then, as fire continues, into actual section losses. Shock and suppression can be rallied off and recovered, but lost sections are gone for good, either casualties or fled. The system deliberately merges morale and losses into a single escalating track.

In Hail of Fire, fire generates Received Fire Points, and when the unit next activates those points are resolved on a table that can leave the unit Ready, Suppressed, or Killed, with hard cover improving its chances. Suppression can be shaken off by rallying or by falling back out of sight, but the cruel twist is that a unit holding even a single unresolved Received Fire Point cannot rally itself; only a leader, or a retreat out of line of sight, can free it.

My analysis: these are two solutions to the same design brief, that fire should pin and degrade before it kills, and that pinned troops should be hard to get moving again. O Group's shock track is a continuous, escalating gradient: more fire means more shock means eventual collapse into casualties, all on one merged scale. Hail of Fire's Received Fire Points are more granular and, crucially, time-delayed: the effect of fire is banked as markers and only revealed later, when you are forced to act. So O Group tells you immediately how shaken a platoon is, by the shock sitting on it, whereas Hail of Fire hides the outcome until activation. Both capture the indefinite pinning of troops under sustained fire, O Group through the difficulty of rallying off accumulated shock, Hail of Fire through the rule that one lingering marker prevents self-rally. If anything, this is the clearest evidence that the two designers share a worldview; they simply chose a continuous track versus a delayed-resolution marker to express it.


## Close combat and assault.

In Hail of Fire, assaults are a tight, alternating sequence. You move into contact, the defenders respond first by rallying and resolving their Received Fire Points, with any non-Ready defender destroyed, and then both sides exchange simple Assault Checks of four or more to destroy a contacted enemy, with leaders granting re-rolls and everyone ending up suppressed afterward. The system reuses the same machinery as the rest of the game and strongly rewards suppressing the objective before you charge it.

O Group treats close combat as a decisive but carefully set-up event. Because it is a command game first, getting a platoon into a successful assault is as much about having spent the orders to suppress the defenders, bring up support, and time the move, as it is about the close-combat dice themselves. Close combat is resolved with attention to the shock and suppression already inflicted, so a defender softened up by fire is far easier to overrun.

My analysis: both games make the same historical argument, that the assault is won before it is launched, in the fire preparation and the maneuver, not in the melee itself. Hail of Fire encodes this very directly and very economically: a defender that has not resolved its Received Fire Points and come up Ready is simply destroyed when contacted, so suppression literally clears the way. O Group encodes the same idea more diffusely, through the cost in orders and the influence of accumulated shock. Hail of Fire's assault is faster and more self-contained; O Group's is more embedded in the larger command and suppression picture. The shared philosophy, fire and maneuver set up the kill, is identical.


## Artillery.

Hail of Fire makes indirect fire central and deliberately friction-laden. You call for fire with your first order of the turn, a marker lands the following turn and scatters a ten-sided die in inches, and you then choose to fire for effect at the scattered point or snap the marker back to its original spot, which neatly models bracketing. Effect is delivered as Received Fire Points by weight, fire missions are rationed across the game, and smoke and sustained barrages are supported.

O Group also places artillery near the center of the experience, as befits a game about how battalions actually fought, where supporting fires often dominated. Barrages from regimental or divisional assets are powerful and characteristically force a hard choice on the target: a unit caught in the open or in cover by such a barrage must either retreat or take additional shock to hold its ground. Forward observation and pre-planned fire are important, and calling and adjusting fire is bound up with the orders economy.

My analysis: both designers clearly believe that artillery is the king of the battlefield, and both make it a matter of timing, observation, and friction rather than a simple template dropped at will. Hail of Fire's signature touches are the one-turn delay, the rationed fire missions, and the elegant scatter-or-snap-back bracketing. O Group's signature touch is the way a barrage attacks the enemy's will to hold ground, forcing the retreat-or-take-more-shock decision, which dovetails perfectly with its shock-based suppression model. O Group's artillery is the more dominant and the more integral to the battle's shape, reflecting the higher echelon, where fire support is a battalion commander's main tool. Hail of Fire's is more rationed and more abstract, reflecting the company commander's more limited call on the guns.


## Morale and the breaking of the force.

Hail of Fire uses two layers. Locally, a platoon that drops below half strength must test or be removed, and a platoon reduced to a single team is simply destroyed. Globally, a Break Point system accumulates as units break and objectives are lost, and when the total exceeds the Break Limit, five plus two per unit, the whole force routs and the game ends.

O Group folds morale and losses together rather than separating them. The same escalating track of shock, then suppression, then section loss that governs suppression also is the morale system; a platoon does not so much fail a morale check as gradually come apart under accumulating shock and casualties. At the battalion level, the erosion is expressed through the command system as much as anything: as the battalion takes casualties, the headquarters loses orders dice, so a failing battalion visibly loses its grip, fielding fewer and fewer effective actions until it can no longer prosecute the battle.

My analysis: here the two games make a subtly different choice. Hail of Fire separates the suppression track, Received Fire Points, from the force-break track, Break Points, giving you two distinct systems, one for the moment-to-moment state of a unit and one for the culmination of the whole force. O Group merges everything into one continuous degradation, where the same shock that suppresses a platoon eventually destroys it, and where the battalion's collapse shows up as a thinning orders pool. O Group's merged approach is arguably the more elegant and the more organic, there is one curve of decline, not two systems to track, and it ties the force's death directly to the command engine. Hail of Fire's split approach is a little more bookkeeping, two kinds of marker, but it gives a cleaner, more game-able sense of exactly how close the whole force is to routing, through an explicit Break Limit number. Both, importantly, model the same essential idea that both share with very few other games: that a force loses by losing cohesion and command, not merely by being killed to the last man.


## Complexity, scale-feel, and playing time.

Hail of Fire is the lighter, faster game, by design. It bills itself as teachable in twenty minutes and playable in a couple of hours, with a two-page reference sheet, a single core mechanic, and a deliberately small component footprint. It gives you the feel of commanding a company in an afternoon.

O Group is the meatier, more deliberate game, again by design. It is a larger canvas, a whole battalion with its supporting arms, a richer and more hierarchical command layer with battalion and company headquarters, and a correspondingly longer and more involved play experience. It is, as its own community often says, a game of command more than of combat, and it rewards the patience to fight a battalion action the way a real commander would.

My analysis: this is the cleanest practical distinction between them. They are not really competing for the same evening. Hail of Fire is the choice when you want the fog of war, the suppression, and the command friction distilled into a quick, elegant company fight. O Group is the choice when you want to sink into a full battalion action, with the reconnaissance battle, the orders triage, and the weight of supporting fires given room to breathe. One is a sharp, fast tactical game with unusually deep fog of war; the other is a command simulation that happens to be played with miniatures.


## The synthesis.

Hail of Fire and O Group are, beneath their different scales, two expressions of the same design creed. Both insist that fire suppresses before it kills. Both drown the player in fog of war and make reconnaissance and uncertainty central. Both make command a scarce resource that erodes under fire, so that a beaten force becomes harder to command as well as weaker. And both turn away from points-list balance toward forces that resemble history.

Where they differ is instructive. Hail of Fire's great original contribution is the delayed resolution of fire, the Received Fire Points that hide your own casualties until you act, which gives it a kind of fog of war about oneself that even O Group does not attempt. O Group's great strengths are its uncompromising reconnaissance-gated spotting, its richly hierarchical and self-degrading command engine, and its merging of suppression, casualties, and morale into a single organic curve of decline. Hail of Fire is the more elegant and the faster; O Group is the deeper and the more authentically a game of command.

If you imagine the two on a shelf, the honest recommendation is not either-or but both-and. Play Hail of Fire when you want a company commander's afternoon: quick, foggy, and decisive. Play O Group when you want a battalion commander's day: the patient reconnaissance, the triage of orders, and the dominance of the guns. They are close enough in philosophy that a player who loves one will almost certainly respect the other, and different enough in scale and weight that owning both leaves no real overlap. They are, in the end, the same good idea about what World War Two combat felt like, told once at the company and once at the battalion.
