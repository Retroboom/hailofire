# Hail of Fire: Audit, Review, and Comparison

A narration-friendly review of Hail of Fire, version 2026 point 06 Beta v3, followed by an in-depth comparison with Flames of War, Battlegroup, and Chain of Command. This document was written to be read aloud, so tables have been rewritten as spoken prose.


## Part One. Audit and Review of Hail of Fire.


### What the game is.

Hail of Fire is a company-level World War Two miniatures game, intended for forces of roughly five to eight platoons per side, using fifteen-millimeter figures or smaller. Infantry are based as teams of two to five men. Vehicles are based individually. The game is built around a small number of unified mechanics. Almost everything resolves on a six-sided die trying to hit four or more, and all modifiers are applied to the target number rather than to the die roll. So if you need a four and you take a plus-one penalty, you now need a five.

The game describes itself as fast play, teachable in twenty minutes, point-free, and scalable from solo play up to multiplayer. It is explicitly designed to work with the orders of battle and scenarios from other games.

The design has a clear, coherent thesis, stated openly in the designer's notes. Fire suppresses far more often than it kills, and a commander rarely knows the true effect of his fire, or even the real state of his own men, until someone is forced to act. Nearly every signature mechanic in the game serves that thesis.


### The core engine.

First, the activation economy, built from Order Points and Hero Points. At the start of a turn you roll one die for each Platoon Leader and each Vehicle Unit. Every roll of four or more gives one Order Point, and you always add one more. Importantly, leaders that are suppressed or shot up contribute no die, so a damaged force generates fewer activations. Hero Points are a secret, variable resource that lets you interrupt, re-activate units, and even act during the opponent's turn.

My verdict on this: it is elegant friction. The variable activation count, combined with the rule that damaged units stop generating dice, models a degrading chain of command very well. The guaranteed plus-one prevents total paralysis. The risk is a death spiral. A losing player loses leaders, then loses dice, then loses tempo, and the problem compounds. This is intentional and thematic, but in lopsided games it can feel punishing.

Second, variable movement used as a kind of overwatch substitute. Infantry move two dice in inches, gun teams one die, and vehicles three dice. You declare your destination first, then roll. Out of line of sight you simply move your maximum. This is explicitly a replacement for reaction fire inside an I-go-you-go structure. Short dashes are reliable. Long sprints across open ground are dangerous. My verdict: this is clever and genuinely original as a friction device. The tradeoff is that movement is decoupled from real vehicle speeds. A Tiger and a Stuart both roll three dice, separated only by the Slow and Fast keywords. This is the least simulationist choice in the system, but it serves playability.

Third, and most important, Received Fire Points, which I will call RFPs. This is the signature mechanic. Hits do not kill immediately. Instead they become RFP markers, and they are resolved only when that team next activates, by rolling one die per point on a Killed, Suppressed, or Ready table. A hit represents effective firepower concentrated on a position, not a bullet striking a man. My verdict: this is the standout idea in the entire game, and the thing that most separates it from its competitors. A single mechanic does three jobs at once. It creates fog of war about your own casualties. It functions as a suppression model. And it controls pacing and tension, because you commit to action without knowing your true state. The related rule, that a single unresolved RFP prevents a suppressed team from rallying itself, is a smart and realistic wrinkle. The four-inch splash, where hits spill over to nearby teams even from other units, rewards dispersion and punishes bunching, which is again historically apt.

Fourth, the armor penetration rule, which I call roll-the-difference. Compare the weapon's anti-tank value to the target's armor rating. If anti-tank is less than or equal to armor, you roll a number of dice equal to the anti-tank value, and any six inflicts received fire points only. If anti-tank is greater than armor, you roll dice equal to the difference, and any six destroys the vehicle. If armor is at least double the anti-tank value, the weapon is simply ineffective. My verdict: this is the most elegant armor rule I have seen at this scale. It captures frontal invulnerability, the marginal zone where a weapon can hurt but rarely kill, and clean kills, all in a single comparison with no charts, and it feeds back into the same RFP currency.

Fifth, the two loss layers, morale and break points. At the unit level, a platoon that drops below half strength must take a Morale Check, and may be removed. Separately, a force-wide Break Point system generates rolls when objectives are lost or units are broken or destroyed. When your accumulated Break Points exceed your Break Limit, which is five plus two per unit, your whole force routs and the game ends. My verdict: both systems work and they do different jobs, local unit collapse versus whole-force culmination. The mild redundancy, where a unit crossing the half-strength line can trigger a Morale Check, and a Break Point roll, and possibly automatic destruction if reduced to a single team, is a teaching hurdle. New players will need that sequence spelled out.


### Audit findings.

Now the genuine issues worth addressing.

Issue one, low severity, a clarity item. Target numbers can exceed six and become impossible. The base is four. Concealment adds one, long range adds one, and a bunker adds one, which produces a seven, impossible on a six-sided die. This is almost certainly intended, meaning you must close the range, but the rules never state that a target number above six is simply unachievable. One sentence would prevent table arguments.

Issue two, trivial. Terminology drift. The Received Fire Point table calls a situation No Hard Cover, while the reference sheet calls the same row Open Ground. Pick one term.

Issue three, low severity. The Elite and Poor troop quality is asymmetric when firing at vehicles. Re-rolling ones, which Elite troops do, is only a small benefit on a roll that wants sixes. But Poor troops re-roll sixes and take the new result, which is a large penalty. The magnitudes are uneven. Worth a conscious decision.

Issue four, medium severity, a clarity problem. The two-layer loss system is not presented as a single ordered checklist. The interaction of the Morale Check, the Break Point rolls, and the automatic destruction of a unit reduced to one team should be laid out in order, especially given that teaching speed is one of the game's stated priorities.

Issue five, medium severity, a balance concern. Hero Point allocation at the start is pure opening luck. Each side secretly rolls one die for starting Hero Points, so a swing of six versus one materially affects how many interrupts a player gets. The Force Parity procedure can hand Hero Points to an opponent to compensate, but the baseline asymmetry in an otherwise balanced match is uncompensated. The designer might consider a floor, a smaller die, or rolling and re-rolling the lower result.

Issue six, low severity, a clarity item. Artillery sequencing has a tension. Resolve Artillery is step one of the turn, but Call for Fire must be the first Order Point spent, which is step four. New players conflate calling a new barrage with resolving a pending one. A short example would close the gap.

Issue seven, low severity, a clarity item. The instruction to return the marker to its original position at Fire for Effect could be misread. The intent, bracketing fire by choosing the better of two points, is good, but the wording could be read as meaning no deviation ever happens.

A few non-issues, so the designer does not waste time. Strange strings like Ri-slash-MG with missing letters are ligature drops caused by extracting text from the PDF. They are not typos in the actual document, and they render correctly on the page. There is, however, one real accessibility note. The designer should confirm that the f-i and f-l ligatures are mapped for copy and paste and for screen readers, because text extraction currently loses those letters, which hurts accessibility.


### Profile and balance spot-check.

I sampled the arsenals and the relative scaling is sound and historically defensible. The armor and anti-tank curve is sensibly compressed. A Sherman sits at front two, flank one, anti-tank four. A Panther is front four, flank two, anti-tank six. A Tiger one is front four, flank three, anti-tank six. A Tiger two is front six, flank three, anti-tank seven. A Jagdtiger tops the scale at front seven, flank three, anti-tank eight. The armor-doubling immunity rule makes the heavy tanks frontally invulnerable to light anti-tank weapons, exactly as expected, while flank shots using roll-the-difference keep them killable. Heavy guns correctly trade rate of fire for power. The Churchill seven, at front six and flank three but only anti-tank four, is a nice historical touch, reflecting heavy armor with a modest gun. No profile struck me as broken. The bigger balance lever is the subjective Force Parity procedure, not the statistics.


### Bottom line on the game itself.

This is a mature, tightly reasoned design, and the claimed ten years of development show. The Received Fire Point mechanic and the armor penetration rule are genuinely excellent, and the whole system collapses onto a two-page reference sheet without feeling thin. Its weaknesses are opening luck variance from Hero Points, the subjectivity of point-free balancing for competitive play, and a small cluster of clarity gaps around loss sequencing and ceiling target numbers. None are structural. All are editable.


## Part Two. Comparative Analysis.

The natural peer group is Flames of War, currently in its fourth edition from Battlefront; Battlegroup, from Iron Fist Publishing, designed by Warwick Kinrade; and Chain of Command, from Too Fat Lardies. One framing note up front. Hail of Fire, Flames of War, and Battlegroup are all company scale. Chain of Command is a reinforced platoon, which is a smaller scale. So Hail of Fire competes with Flames of War and Battlegroup on scope, but it shares its design philosophy, the emphasis on friction, fog of war, and leadership, most closely with Chain of Command.


### Section A. Mechanics.

On turn structure. Hail of Fire uses an I-go-you-go base, but with a variable number of Order Points and with Hero Point interrupts layered on top. Flames of War uses strict I-go-you-go with fixed phases for movement, shooting, and assault. Battlegroup uses alternating activations driven by rolling two dice for orders, with reactive fire from units on ambush. Chain of Command uses Command Dice, rolling five or six dice each turn, where results unlock activations and multiples grant bonus phases.

On the feel of activation. Hail of Fire makes tempo a resource through its semi-random activation count. Flames of War is deterministic and almost chess-like. Battlegroup is an interactive order economy. Chain of Command is highly variable and rich in friction.

On shooting. Hail of Fire rolls rate-of-fire dice needing four or more, with modifiers to the target number, and crucially the hits become delayed received fire points. Flames of War rolls to hit using skill, then takes an armor save, then a firepower test. Battlegroup rolls to hit, then resolves penetration using real armor and gun penetration values from tables, then damage. Chain of Command rolls big dice pools that produce kills and accumulating shock.

On armor. Hail of Fire uses single-digit anti-tank versus armor with the roll-the-difference method. Flames of War uses front, side, and top saves against anti-tank, plus a firepower test. Battlegroup uses actual armor thickness in millimeters against gun penetration, which is table driven. Chain of Command abstracts armor heavily, emphasizing shock and morale over ballistics.

On casualty timing, the key differentiator. Hail of Fire delays resolution until the next activation. Flames of War, Battlegroup, and Chain of Command all resolve casualties immediately, though Chain of Command's shock delays effectiveness rather than knowledge.

My reading: Hail of Fire's engine is the most economical. One resolution mechanic covers shooting, morale, terrain, and assault, and the received fire point currency unifies infantry suppression with vehicle damage. Battlegroup is the most granular. Flames of War is the most structured and predictable. Chain of Command has the richest activation engine but the heaviest command overhead. The thing no competitor has is Hail of Fire's delayed casualty resolution. Battlegroup and Flames of War tell you immediately what died. Chain of Command's shock delays capability but not knowledge. Hail of Fire uniquely hides your own losses until you act.


### Section B. Historical accuracy.

Two distinct senses of accuracy matter here. Technical fidelity, meaning equipment and orders of battle, and doctrinal fidelity, meaning tactics and command.

Battlegroup is the grognard's choice for technical accuracy, with real armor thicknesses, gun penetration that varies by range, theater-specific army lists, and scenario books that reconstruct actual engagements.

Flames of War has well-researched orders of battle and force organizations, but its mechanics are deliberately gamey, and its tournament environment produces optimized lists that can be ahistorical in composition. Accurate models and rosters, but game-first resolution.

Chain of Command is strongest on doctrinal accuracy, with realistic platoon organizations, the centrality of junior leaders, national command styles, and authentic small-unit tactics. It abstracts equipment performance because it is a game about men and command, not ballistics.

Hail of Fire sits with Chain of Command philosophically. Its stated goal is that authentic tactics produce historical results. Its orders of battle are representative and simplified, with a default three-squad platoon and half-squad teams, and armor abstracted to single digits. So it is not trying to match Battlegroup's technical fidelity. Where it earns real historical credibility is in its model of fire effects. The premise, that suppression is the real product of fire and that something like forty thousand rounds were fired per soldier killed, reflects current scholarship, including the suppression-centric view found in works like Leo Murray's Brains and Bullets. And it is baked into the core loop rather than bolted on as flavor. The optional National Characteristics, such as the British Mike Target artillery rule, German mission tactics with an extra Order Point, Soviet massed assault and a follow-the-leader command rigidity rule, and American semi-automatic firepower and time-on-target artillery, are well chosen and doctrinally literate. Crucially they are optional and caveated. The Soviet rigidity rule explicitly notes that it is a pre-nineteen-forty-four cliché. That restraint signals a designer who knows the historiography.

To rank by sense: for technical fidelity, Battlegroup leads, then Flames of War and Hail of Fire roughly together, then Chain of Command. For doctrinal and tactical fidelity, Chain of Command and Hail of Fire lead together, then Battlegroup, then Flames of War.


### Section C. Realism, meaning process simulation.

The question here is whether the experience feels like command. This is where Hail of Fire is most ambitious. It models several things the others mostly do not. It models uncertainty about your own force's state, because received fire points are unresolved until you act. This is arguably the most realistic friction in any of these games. Battlegroup and Flames of War give you a god's-eye casualty report. Hail of Fire makes you commit blind. It models fire as suppression rather than lethality by default. Chain of Command's shock is comparable, but Hail of Fire's rule that a single point pins men indefinitely without a leader is a sharper and more accurate model of how hard it is to get pinned men moving. It models command friction through variable Order Points and the leader-attrition feedback loop. And it stacks fog of war deeply, through hidden teams, variable movement outcomes, secret Hero Points, secret Break Points, and recon-extended reveal ranges.

Against that, Battlegroup wins on ballistic and equipment realism, because penetration, gun handling, and vehicle reliability are simulated, not merely keyworded. Its Battle Rating chit-pull elegantly hides when the enemy will break. Chain of Command matches Hail of Fire on command and morale realism and exceeds it on pre-battle reconnaissance, because its Patrol Phase is a masterpiece of abstracted scouting. Flames of War is the least concerned with process realism, by design.

The honest tradeoff in Hail of Fire is its movement abstraction, where everyone of a given type rolls the same dice. That is the one place where playability clearly beats realism. Battlegroup and Chain of Command tie movement more tightly to real capability.

To summarize: for command-experience realism, meaning friction, fog, and fire effect, Hail of Fire and Chain of Command lead together, then Battlegroup, then Flames of War. For physical and equipment realism, Battlegroup leads, then Flames of War and Hail of Fire together, then Chain of Command.


### Section D. Design elegance.

This is Hail of Fire's strongest dimension. Hail of Fire achieves company-scale combined arms with one core roll, four or more with modifiers to the target, one unified damage currency in received fire points spanning both infantry and armor, a charts-free penetration rule, and a complete two-page reference sheet. Multiple mechanics each do double or triple duty. Variable movement is both overwatch and friction. Received fire points are casualties, suppression, pacing, and fog all at once. It is teachable in twenty minutes as advertised, and the rules prose is disciplined, with designer's notes that justify rather than pad. The cost of that elegance is precision, given the point-free balancing and abstracted movement.

Chain of Command has an elegant engine in its Command Dice and Patrol Phase, but a famously discursive and hard-to-reference rulebook. Elegant ideas, less elegant presentation. Flames of War is moderately elegant at its core but accumulates special rules, unit cards, and phase overhead, and rules bloat is a recurring complaint. Battlegroup explicitly trades elegance for fidelity, with tables, subsystems, and lookups. It is rewarding but the heaviest of the four to teach and run.

Ranking elegance: Hail of Fire first, then Chain of Command for its engine, then Flames of War, then Battlegroup.


### Section E. Where each game wins.

Choose Flames of War for a deep model and army ecosystem, tournament structure, points-precise list building, and predictable rules you can play competitively.

Choose Battlegroup for maximum technical and ballistic realism, historical scenario reconstruction, and for players who actively enjoy detail.

Choose Chain of Command for the best small-unit command and leadership experience, and the best pre-battle scouting, at platoon scale.

Choose Hail of Fire for company-scale combined arms with the fog of war and fire-suppression realism of Chain of Command, the attritional culmination feel of Battlegroup's chit-pull through its Break Points, and the fastest and most elegant ruleset of the four. It also offers genuinely distinctive perks. It is free. It uses point-free pickup balancing. It needs few components, just six-sided dice, one ten-sided die, and tokens. It supports solo and multiplayer play. And it is deliberately built to interoperate with the figures, orders of battle, and scenarios of other games.


### The final verdict.

Hail of Fire is best understood as Chain of Command's command-friction and fire-effect philosophy, scaled up to company level, combined with Battlegroup-style force attrition through Break Points, and delivered in the most elegant ruleset of the group. Its original contributions, the delayed received fire point resolution and the roll-the-difference penetration rule, are strong enough to stand alongside the established three. It deliberately concedes Battlegroup's ballistic granularity and Flames of War's tournament-grade points precision. In exchange it offers speed, teachability, deep fog of war, and a coherent design thesis that almost every rule actually serves. For its target audience, players who want authentic World War Two decisions in two hours without charts, it is arguably the most successful of the four. The remaining rough edges are editorial and balance tuning, not architectural.
