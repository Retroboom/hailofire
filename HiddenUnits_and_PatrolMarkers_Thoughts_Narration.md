# Hidden Units, Patrol Markers, and Scale: Thoughts

A narration-friendly, read-aloud response for ElevenReader.


## First, a correction on my part.

You are right, and I owe you an accurate restatement. In my earlier suggestions I leaned on a flawed picture of how Hail of Fire handles hidden units, so let me set it straight before I give you my actual opinion.

Hail of Fire's hidden units are not pre-plotted. There is no map, no written deployment, no secret list of grid references. A hidden team is not even on the table. It exists as a potential that the controlling player collapses into a real position at the moment of their choosing, subject only to the reveal constraints: a hidden team must be revealed inside its own deployment zone, and either out of the enemy's line of sight, or in concealing terrain at least sixteen inches from enemy infantry, guns, and open-topped vehicles, or eight inches from other vehicles. If the enemy has just moved, you subtract the distance they moved from those stand-off ranges, and a position that was out of sight before the enemy moved can be revealed freely. So the system is, in a sense, a floating reserve of concealment that the defender spends when it is most valuable.

And, crucially, this is not an accident or a shortcut that happens to be playable. It is a deliberately designed meta-game. The defender wants to hold their hidden teams until the perfect moment. The attacker cannot go looking for them on a map, because there is no map; instead the attacker maneuvers to shrink the set of good, legal places the defender can still reveal into. By advancing carefully, threatening the obvious ambush positions, and closing the distance, the attacker forces the defender to either reveal early, at long range and to little effect, or to gamble on holding and risk having no good reveal left at all. The whole thing is, as you put it, not realistic in its procedure, but it produces realistic results, and it generates a genuine cat-and-mouse contest between hidden defenders and probing attackers. I under-credited that, and my earlier recon-gating idea was partly built on the wrong mental model, since you cannot fire at a hidden team that is not on the table in the first place. So thank you for the correction.


## A point of attribution, in fairness.

It is worth a small note that patrol markers, as a named pre-game mechanic where markers advance, lock each other down, and then fix the jump-off points you deploy from, are most strongly associated with Chain of Command, which comes from the same broad family of designers as O Group. O Group's own fog of war leans more on hidden deployment and reconnaissance-gated spotting than on that specific patrol-marker phase. So when we talk about borrowing patrol markers, we are really talking about that Lardies-family pre-game scouting idea in general. I want to be precise about that rather than put a mechanic in O Group's mouth that may not be exactly there.


## Now, my actual opinion. I think your instinct is correct.

I believe Hail of Fire's markerless, no-map, no-pre-commitment approach is genuinely the better fit for its scale and its identity, and that importing patrol markers would cost more than it would add. Let me give you the reasoning, because I think the why matters more than the verdict.

The first reason is philosophical consistency. Hail of Fire's entire design signature is abstract in procedure, realistic in result. The Received Fire Points do this with casualties. The roll-then-move distances do this with movement under fire. The Force Parity Procedure does this with balance. The hidden-unit system is the same idea applied to concealment: you do not simulate the procedure of digging in and camouflaging a position, you simulate the result, which is that a defender's hidden strength reveals itself at a moment and place of the defender's choosing, within believable limits. Patrol markers are the opposite philosophy. They are realistic in procedure: you physically scout, your patrols contest ground, and where they end up determines where you can be. Grafting a procedure-realistic subsystem onto a results-realistic game would create a tonal seam, a place where the game suddenly asks you to simulate the process rather than the outcome. That seam would be felt, and it would slightly betray the rest of the design.

The second reason is what patrol markers actually do, and when they pay off. Patrol markers do their work before the game, by solving the problem of where forces may deploy. They shine when that deployment problem is large and central: when the table is big relative to the forces, when deployment zones are not simple and obvious, and when the scouting battle for ground is itself a major part of the interest of the engagement. That is true at Chain of Command's tight platoon scale, where the patrol phase is a real fraction of the whole tactical experience, and it is true at O Group's battalion scale, where there is a great deal of ground and a real reconnaissance screen to fight over. Hail of Fire sits in between, as a fast company game on a six-by-four table with a handful of units, explicitly built to teach in twenty minutes and finish in a couple of hours. At that scale, a pre-game patrol phase has a poor ratio of overhead to payoff. It adds components, it adds a whole phase with its own rules, and it eats a meaningful slice of a short game, all to solve a deployment problem that Hail of Fire keeps deliberately simple.

The third reason is the one I find most persuasive. Patrol markers front-load the fog of war into the setup and then largely resolve it. Once the jump-off points are fixed, much of the uncertainty about where forces are has been spent. Hail of Fire's floating-reveal system does the opposite: it keeps the fog alive across the entire game. On every single turn, the defender is weighing reveal now or hold, and the attacker is maneuvering to force a bad reveal. That ongoing, turn-by-turn tension is more valuable in a fast game than a rich but one-time setup ritual, because it keeps paying out for the whole two hours rather than front-loading the interest into the first fifteen minutes. In other words, patrol markers and Hail of Fire's floating system are partly substitutes, two different ways to deliver fog of war, and Hail of Fire's version is better tuned to a short game because the tension is continuous rather than spent up front.


## Where the floating system is genuinely weaker, in honesty.

I do not want to be a cheerleader, so here are its real soft spots. It can feel counterintuitive to a new player, who reasonably asks where their units actually are. It can occasionally produce a reveal that feels less like they were dug in there all along and more like they materialized at the last legal inch, if a clever defender games the stand-off distances. And it is a single layer of fog: it hides the enemy's position, but it does not really model the effort of scouting to find them. There is not, in the base game, a true reconnaissance battle so much as a maneuver-and-threat battle. The attacker contests the fog indirectly, by maneuver, rather than by actively scouting.

But here is the thing worth noticing: Hail of Fire already addresses that gap in embryo, and it does so in its own markerless idiom rather than with patrol markers. A reconnaissance team forces hidden units to reveal at twenty inches from it, instead of the usual sixteen. That single rule turns recon into a counter-ambush screen: pushing a recon team forward shrinks the defender's good reveal options and forces them to show themselves further away and to less effect. That is precisely the attacker agency over the fog that patrol markers are reaching for, delivered with no markers, no map, and no pre-game phase. So the design instinct you already have, of contesting hidden units through maneuver and reconnaissance rather than through a setup ritual, is sound and is already present.


## So, my recommendation.

I would not adopt patrol markers. I think they are the right tool for Chain of Command's and O Group's scales and the wrong tool for yours, and I think your default of no markers, no maps, and no pre-deciding is not a compromise but actually the stronger choice for a fast company game, because it is philosophically consistent, it has near-zero overhead, and it keeps the fog of war live for the whole game instead of spending it at setup.

If you ever feel the floating system needs more of a scouting dimension, the move is to deepen the lever you already have, not to import a new subsystem. For example, as a purely optional rule, you could let a reconnaissance team that ends its activation with clear line of sight to a piece of likely cover deny a free reveal in that area for a turn, representing eyes actually on the ground, so the defender must either reveal now, while the scout is watching and can react, or wait and let the attacker consolidate. That would sharpen the attacker's ability to flush hidden units, it would reward using recon aggressively, and it would do all of it in Hail of Fire's existing language of distances, line of sight, and reveal timing, with no markers and no pre-game phase. But that is a small, optional refinement of a system that, in my honest assessment, is already well-matched to the game it lives in.

The short version is this. Patrol markers are a beautiful solution to a problem Hail of Fire does not really have, at a scale Hail of Fire does not occupy, in a design philosophy Hail of Fire does not share. Your markerless floating reveal is the more elegant answer for a fast company game, and the right place to invest, if you invest at all, is in deepening reconnaissance's ability to pressure the fog, not in replacing the fog with a setup ritual.
