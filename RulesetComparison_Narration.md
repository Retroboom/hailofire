# Comparing Hail of Fire's Systems with Other Popular Rulesets

A narration-friendly, read-aloud version. This document compares Hail of Fire, subsystem by subsystem, with the other major World War Two miniatures rulesets that cover the same ground: Flames of War, fourth edition; Bolt Action, third edition from twenty twenty-four; Battlegroup; and Chain of Command. It also brings in two influential outliers, Crossfire and O Group, at the points where they best illuminate what Hail of Fire is doing. Tables have been rewritten as spoken prose, and dice values are written out, so a text-to-speech reader can narrate it smoothly.


## A note on scale.

Before the comparison, a framing point, because it shapes everything. Hail of Fire, Flames of War, Bolt Action, and Battlegroup are all company-level games. Chain of Command is a reinforced platoon, a notch smaller. Crossfire is company-level. O Group is battalion-level, a notch larger. So Hail of Fire's most direct mechanical rivals are Flames of War, Bolt Action, and Battlegroup, while Chain of Command, Crossfire, and O Group are better understood as its philosophical cousins, sharing its interest in command friction, suppression, and fog of war.


## Section One. Force Construction.

Hail of Fire is the odd one out here, and deliberately so: it is point-free. Units are platoons, with a default of three squads, three guns, or three vehicles. Squads are split into roughly half-squad teams. Infantry and gun platoons get a platoon leader. You then add zero to three support weapons. Balance does not come from a points list. Instead, it comes from the Force Parity Procedure, a comparative, subjective ranking and pairing of the two forces, where any imbalance is converted into starting Hero Points for the weaker side or Break Points against the stronger side. The force's overall staying power is its Break Limit, which is five plus two for every unit. The companion app adds a hidden points formula and a simulator, but the ruleset itself has no points at all.

Flames of War and Bolt Action are the opposite pole. Both use rigid points systems, with published costs and theater-based force selectors. In Flames of War, the core organizational block, the Formation, also serves as the command and morale backbone. This approach is precise and tournament-ready, but it is ahistorical at the margins, because points optimization tends to produce armies that no real quartermaster would recognize.

Battlegroup uses points plus a Battle Rating. Every unit contributes to a force's Battle Rating, which is at once its build budget and its breaking point. Its force lists are tightly tied to specific theaters and dates.

Chain of Command barely uses points at all. You field a fixed platoon from a national list, and then choose support on a sliding scale tied to how lopsided the scenario is, so the underdog receives more support. This is the most scenario-driven and least list-building-focused approach of the group.

My analysis: Hail of Fire's point-free design is its boldest construction choice, and the one most aligned with real force organization. You field a platoon because platoons existed, not because they cost two hundred forty-seven points. The cost of this approach is precision. Subjective parity invites table arguments and cannot support a tournament scene the way Flames of War and Bolt Action can. Chain of Command's scenario-scaled support is the closest peer in philosophy, but Hail of Fire is unique in making balance itself a pre-game negotiation rather than a fixed number. Of all these games, Hail of Fire and Chain of Command produce the most believable orders of battle, and Flames of War and Bolt Action the least.


## Section Two. Activation and the Turn Sequence.

This is the engine everything else hangs on, so it is worth covering early.

Hail of Fire runs a variable-tempo, you-go-then-I-go structure. Each turn you roll one die for each platoon leader and each vehicle unit. Every roll of four or more yields one Order Point, and you always add one more for free. Crucially, leaders who have been shot up generate no die, so a damaged force gets fewer actions, creating a kind of command death-spiral. On top of this sit Hero Points, a secret and variable resource that lets you interrupt, re-activate units, and even act during the enemy's turn. A leader spends one Order Point to move or fire a whole platoon, or splits two orders within it.

Flames of War uses a strict, deterministic you-go-then-I-go structure with phases: you move, shoot, and assault with your entire army, then your opponent does the same. It is predictable and low-friction, almost chess-like, but the player who is not active is largely a spectator.

Bolt Action uses its famous order-dice bag. One die per unit goes into a bag, dice are drawn at random, and the player who owns the drawn die activates that unit with one of six possible orders. Activation is alternating and unpredictable; you never fully control the sequence. It produces the most tension per activation of any mainstream game.

Battlegroup has you roll two dice, modified by command, for a number of Orders, which you spend to activate units one at a time, alternating, with officers granting extra orders within their command range. This is an order economy much like Hail of Fire's, but with reactive opportunity fire built in.

Chain of Command uses Command Dice. You roll five or six six-sided dice; ones activate teams, higher numbers activate leaders by rank, and sixes are banked toward a special Chain of Command die. Rolling matching sets grants extra phases or ends the turn. It is the richest and most friction-heavy activation engine of all of them.

Crossfire is the radical one. It has no turn sequence at all. You keep activating until you fail at something, or until enemy reactive fire stops you, and then initiative passes to your opponent. It is pure fire-and-maneuver tempo.

My analysis: Hail of Fire sits deliberately between the rigidity of Flames of War and the chaos of Chain of Command and Bolt Action. Its Order-Point economy is closest to Battlegroup. Its Hero-Point interrupt layer imports Chain of Command-like unpredictability without that game's overhead. And the leader-attrition feedback, where fewer leaders mean fewer dice, is a genuinely elegant friction touch that none of the mainstream four model so directly. The trade-off is that Hail of Fire is still fundamentally a game where I act and then you act, so it is less reactive than Battlegroup's opportunity fire or Crossfire's continuous initiative. It compensates for that through variable movement rather than interrupt fire, which is the next subsystem.


## Section Three. Movement.

Here is one of Hail of Fire's signature ideas: declared-then-rolled variable distance. Infantry move two six-sided dice in inches, gun teams one die, and vehicles three dice. You declare your destination first, then roll. If the whole move is out of the enemy's line of sight, you simply move your maximum. The designer calls this a pseudo-overwatch. Short dashes are reliable, while long sprints across the open are gambles, because the shorter the move, the more likely you complete it before the enemy's turn lets them fire at you. Vehicles pivot freely in their first six inches, then must move straight; rough ground requires terrain checks; and roads grant bonuses.

Flames of War, Bolt Action, Battlegroup, and Chain of Command all use fixed movement rates, with tactical and dash or run distinctions and terrain cross-checks. Distance is deterministic. The risk comes from what you move into, namely defensive or opportunity fire, not from whether you get there.

Crossfire uses no measurement at all. You bound from one terrain feature to the next, as far as you like, until reactive fire stops you. The risk is entirely reactive.

My analysis: Hail of Fire and Crossfire are the only two of these games that make movement itself uncertain, and they do it for the same reason, to inject the risk of advancing under fire into a system that has no reaction fire, in Hail of Fire's case, or that uses it as the whole engine, in Crossfire's case. Hail of Fire's version is more wargamer-friendly, because you still measure; you just do not know your roll in advance. And it cleverly removes the single fiddliest thing in you-go-then-I-go games, which is interrupting the opponent's move to shoot at them. The realism cost is that distance is decoupled from real vehicle speed. A Tiger and a Stuart both roll three dice, differentiated only by the Slow and Fast keywords, where Battlegroup and Chain of Command tie movement more tightly to capability. It is a clean, conscious trade of geometric realism for playability.


## Section Four. Shooting, including Anti-Tank Fire.

In Hail of Fire, you roll a number of dice equal to your weapon's Rate of Fire, you hit on four or more, and all modifiers are applied to the target number rather than to the die. So a concealed target adds one, long range adds one, and a concealed target at long range now needs a six. Critically, hits do not kill. They become Received Fire Points, resolved later, which is the subject of the next section. Fire is directed at a position: you need line of sight to the target, or to a team within four inches of it. And hits splash to any team within four inches, even across unit boundaries, which encourages dispersion.

Anti-tank fire is the elegant centerpiece. You compare the weapon's Anti-Tank value to the target's Armor Rating. If Anti-Tank is less than or equal to Armor, you roll a number of dice equal to the Anti-Tank value, and any six inflicts suppression only. If Anti-Tank is greater than Armor, you roll dice equal to the difference, and any six destroys the vehicle. And if Armor is at least double the Anti-Tank value, the weapon simply cannot hurt it. One comparison, no charts, and it folds straight back into the Received Fire Point currency.

Flames of War uses a to-hit roll based on skill, then a two-step kill for armor: an armor save against the anti-tank value, then a firepower test to confirm the kill, with infantry taking their own saves. It is robust but involves multiple rolls.

Bolt Action uses a to-hit roll, heavily modified by range, cover, and orders, then a damage step. Infantry mostly just die per hit, while vehicles use penetration against armor plus a damage table. Importantly, every hit that does not kill still leaves a pin marker, which is its suppression model.

Battlegroup uses a to-hit roll, then for vehicles it uses real gun-penetration values against armor across range bands, the most ballistically detailed approach of the group, with infantry resolved through beaten zones and saves.

Chain of Command uses big dice pools per weapon, hitting on four or five, producing a mix of kills and Shock at the same time.

My analysis: two things make Hail of Fire distinctive. First, modifiers hit the target number, not the dice. This is a small but genuinely elegant simplification that keeps a single resolution mechanic everywhere in the game. Second, the roll-the-difference penetration rule is, in my view, the best armor rule at this scale. It captures frontal invulnerability, the can-hurt-but-rarely-kill band, and clean flank kills in a single comparison, where Flames of War needs two rolls and Battlegroup needs a chart. Battlegroup remains the king of ballistic fidelity, but Hail of Fire wins on elegance per unit of realism. And Hail of Fire's model of firing at a position, with splash within four inches, is a more honest depiction of area suppression than the strictly unit-targeted shooting of Flames of War or Bolt Action.


## Section Five. Suppression and Fire Effects. This is Hail of Fire's signature system.

This is the subsystem where Hail of Fire is most original. Because hits become Received Fire Points, resolved only when the team next activates, by rolling one die per point on a Killed, Suppressed, or Ready table, with hard cover improving the odds, Hail of Fire delivers three things at once that other games handle separately. First, suppression. Second, fog of war about your own casualties, because you do not know what your fire did until you are forced to act on that unit. And third, pacing. The cruelest and cleverest wrinkle is this: a unit with even one unresolved Received Fire Point cannot rally itself. So a mere trickle of fire keeps heads down indefinitely, and only a leader, or falling back out of line of sight, can free them.

Here is how the others model suppression. Bolt Action uses pins: every non-killing hit adds a pin marker, pins subtract from the order test a unit must pass to act, and they stack toward forcing a unit to go Down. It is simple, visible, and effective, and it is the closest mainstream analogue to what Hail of Fire is trying to do. Chain of Command uses Shock, which accumulates on teams alongside kills; when Shock equals the team's strength the team is pinned, and beyond that it breaks, with leaders spending their activations to remove Shock. Flames of War uses a Pinned Down status, where a unit pinned by enough hits moves at half speed and shoots poorly until it un-pins with a motivation test; this is coarser and more binary. Battlegroup uses pinned and suppressed statuses that require officers to rally the troops. And Crossfire makes fire results literally a scale of No Effect, Pinned, Suppressed, or Kill, so suppression is the entire point of shooting, and a suppression transfers the initiative. Philosophically, Crossfire is Hail of Fire's ancestor.

My analysis: every modern World War Two ruleset now agrees that fire suppresses more than it kills. Hail of Fire, Bolt Action, Chain of Command, and Crossfire all build on this, while Flames of War models it most weakly. What makes Hail of Fire unique is delaying the resolution. In Bolt Action and Chain of Command you know immediately how many men fell; in Hail of Fire you do not, and that uncertainty about your own force's state is the most realistic friction in any of these games. The rule that one Received Fire Point pins a unit indefinitely is a sharper, more cynical, and more accurate model of how hard it is to get pinned men moving than anyone else's. The risk Hail of Fire runs is stalemate, which it mitigates through leaders, through the fall-back rule, and through the splash mechanic that keeps fire spreading.


## Section Six. Assaults and Close Combat.

In Hail of Fire, you move into base contact, and you must start within twelve inches, with at least one unsuppressed team that has line of sight. Then there is a strict alternating sequence. Defenders respond first: they rally, they resolve their Received Fire Points, and any defender that is not Ready is destroyed; survivors then either fall back or roll to fight. Then attackers strike. Assault Checks are a simple four or more to destroy a contacted enemy. Leaders grant re-rolls. No more than two attackers may contact one defender. And everyone who fights, or who breaks off, ends up Suppressed afterward, in what the rules call the regroup. Against armor, an infantry assault check counts as a flank hit, with anti-tank value increased by one, or treated as anti-tank two if the team has none, and open-topped vehicles are automatically destroyed if they resolve Received Fire Points while in contact.

Flames of War uses a charge, then defensive fire, then multiple combat rounds, then counterattack and morale tests, with tanks having their own assault rules. It is detailed and swingy. Bolt Action makes assaults deliberately brutal and decisive: you move into contact, you fight, and the loser is wiped out entirely, with no half-measures and no pins taken in melee. It is the most lethal close combat of the group. Chain of Command makes close combat a bloody dice-pool brawl, heavily influenced by numbers and leaders, where assaulting good troops in cover is suicidal. And in Battlegroup, assault is comparatively rare and risky, with defensive fire and morale doing most of the work.

My analysis: Hail of Fire threads a needle. Its assault is decisive, capturing the idea from its own design notes that the last thirty yards are where the enemy is actually killed, but it is not a separate, complex subsystem. It reuses the same four-or-more check and the same Received Fire Point and suppression machinery used everywhere else. The requirement that you first suppress the objective, because Ready defenders fight back hard and you want them resolving Received Fire Points as casualties first, genuinely forces the historical fire-then-close sequence, which Bolt Action's just-charge approach and Flames of War's multi-round grind achieve less elegantly. It is less granular than Chain of Command's brawl, but far cleaner, and the rule that everyone ends up suppressed afterward is a nice touch that prevents unrealistic sweeping advances.


## Section Seven. Artillery.

In Hail of Fire, indirect fire needs at least two guns and an observer. You call for fire with your first Order Point of the turn. A Range-In marker then lands at the start of your next turn and deviates a ten-sided die in inches. You then choose to either Fire for Effect at the deviated point, or snap the marker back to its original position, which is a clever, abstracted way of modeling bracketing fire. The effect is Received Fire Points by weight, one for Light, two for Medium, and three for Heavy, in a radius of four inches or more. Fire missions are rationed per game, four for Light, three for Medium, and two for Heavy. Smoke and sustained barrages are supported, and on-table mortars can observe for themselves. The delay, where you call this turn and the fire lands next turn, is the core friction.

Flames of War, Bolt Action, and Battlegroup all use templates, observers, and ranging-in, with Battlegroup the most detailed, featuring pre-registration and full forward-observer procedures, and Bolt Action the most driven by templates and deviation dice. Flames of War models repeat bombardments and smoke cleanly. Chain of Command delivers artillery through its special Chain of Command die or a pre-planned barrage via a forward observer, which is powerful, friction-laden, and a major reason to bank command dice. And O Group makes artillery and eyes-on spotting central, with strong fog of war about where the fire actually lands.

My analysis: Hail of Fire's artillery is its fiddliest subsystem, the one place where the fast-play promise strains a little. But it is faithful to the two things that matter most about World War Two indirect fire: the time lag, because your fire arrives a turn late, so you must predict where the enemy will be, and rationed availability, because fire missions are finite. The choice to either Fire for Effect at the deviation or snap back to the origin is an elegant abstraction of bracketing that I have not seen elsewhere. It models less technical detail than Battlegroup's forward-observer procedures, but it captures the command experience, predict, wait, adjust, about as well as anything outside O Group, and it folds the result into the same Received Fire Point currency rather than a separate template-casualty system.


## Section Eight. Morale and Force Break.

Hail of Fire runs two layers. There is a local layer, a unit check when a platoon drops below half strength, where failure means the platoon is removed, and a platoon reduced to a single team automatically dies. And there is a force-wide layer, the Break Point system: losing objectives, or having units broken, generates Break Point rolls, and when your total exceeds your Break Limit, which is five plus two per unit, your whole force routs and the game ends.

Battlegroup is the obvious cousin here. Its Battle Rating and battle-counter system is its morale engine. Destroyed or pinned units force you to draw counters that accumulate toward your Battle Rating limit, at which point you withdraw, and the counters also carry fog of war about when the enemy will break. Hail of Fire's Break Points are essentially a streamlined, dice-driven version of the Battle Rating. Flames of War uses Formation morale and Last Stand: formations break when their commander and half their units are gone, and the army folds when its formations do. Bolt Action uses unit-level morale, an order test against a morale value reduced by pins, plus a check at fifty percent casualties, but it has no single force-break track, so games run to objectives or a turn limit. And Chain of Command uses a single Force Morale track, starting around nine to eleven, that degrades from leader losses and broken sections; when it reaches zero, you are done. It is the cleanest single-number force morale of the group.

My analysis: Hail of Fire's two-layer system is a sensible hybrid, combining local unit collapse, like Bolt Action and Flames of War, with a whole-force culmination, like Battlegroup and Chain of Command. The Break Point system specifically borrows Battlegroup's best idea, attritional force collapse with built-in uncertainty, since Break Point totals can be hidden, while replacing the chit-pull bookkeeping with a quick dice roll. The mild redundancy, where a unit crossing the halfway point can trigger a morale check, and a Break Point roll, and possibly automatic destruction all at once, is the one place where Hail of Fire's morale is less clean than Chain of Command's single track, and it is the subsystem most in need of a clear, ordered checklist.


## Section Nine. Command and Fog of War. The cross-cutting theme.

This deserves to be isolated, because it is Hail of Fire's deepest design commitment. Hail of Fire stacks fog of war thicker than any of its company-level rivals. There are secret Hero Points, secret Break Points, hidden deployment, variable movement where you do not know if you will arrive, and, uniquely, delayed casualty resolution, where you do not even know your own losses. Command friction comes from the Order-Point economy and from the leader-attrition spiral.

Chain of Command is the gold standard for command friction, through its Command Dice and its Patrol Phase, a pre-game scouting system that sets up jump-off points, but it still gives you full information about your casualties. O Group rivals Hail of Fire on fog of war, with hidden units, eyes-on spotting, and orders friction, though at a higher echelon. Battlegroup hides when the enemy will break, through its battle counters, and uses reconnaissance and reserves well. And Flames of War and Bolt Action are largely full-information games, where you see everything; Bolt Action's only real fog is the order in which dice are drawn.

My analysis: no other company-level game makes you commit as blind as Hail of Fire does. That is its identity. Chain of Command matches it on command friction, O Group on hidden forces, and Battlegroup on break-uncertainty, but only Hail of Fire hides your own force's status from you, through the Received Fire Point mechanic. That single idea is the throughline connecting all of its other subsystems.


## Synthesis. What Hail of Fire borrows, and what is genuinely its own.

Borrowed and refined: the order-economy activation, from Battlegroup; the interrupt and hero resources, in the spirit of Chain of Command's command dice; suppression-first combat, from Bolt Action's pins, Chain of Command's shock, and Crossfire; attritional force-break, from Battlegroup's Battle Rating, which becomes Hail of Fire's Break Points; and scenario-scaled balance, from Chain of Command, which becomes Hail of Fire's Force Parity.

Genuinely original, or close to it: first, delayed casualty resolution through Received Fire Points, giving you fog of war about your own losses; I cannot name another tabletop game that does this as a core mechanic. Second, the roll-the-difference armor penetration, the most elegant anti-tank rule at this scale. Third, declared-then-rolled variable movement as an overwatch substitute, shared in spirit only with Crossfire, but uniquely measured. Fourth, applying modifiers to the target number rather than the dice, a tiny unifying elegance that pervades every subsystem. And fifth, point-free Force Parity as a pre-game negotiation.

An honest ranking by dimension. For ballistic and technical realism: Battlegroup leads, then Hail of Fire and Flames of War together, then Bolt Action and Chain of Command. For command and fog-of-war realism: Hail of Fire, Chain of Command, and O Group lead together, then Battlegroup, then Flames of War and Bolt Action. For design elegance and speed: Hail of Fire and Bolt Action lead the company-level field, with Chain of Command's engine elegant but its rulebook dense, and Battlegroup the heaviest. And for competitive precision: Flames of War and Bolt Action win on their points systems, while Hail of Fire deliberately opts out.

The one-line verdict: Hail of Fire is best read as a synthesis game. It takes the suppression model the whole hobby has converged on, the attritional force-break that Battlegroup pioneered, and the command friction that Chain of Command perfected, and then it adds its own keystone, delayed Received Fire Point resolution, that ties them all together into the most fog-of-war-forward company-level World War Two ruleset available, at a fraction of the bookkeeping of its simulationist peers.
